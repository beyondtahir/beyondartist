## TIP-01 — Y2K Aesthetic

**Visual system.** The examples connect metallic or inflated display lettering, saturated blue, acidic yellow-green, magenta, black and white with fashion imagery, faux computer windows, stickers, barcodes and starbursts. Shiny fashion photography, cartoon lettering and pixel-art computers coexist. The shared mechanism is energetic early-digital nostalgia. Large type and subjects dominate; tiny labels provide texture.

**Context and limits.** Consider music, playful fashion or nostalgic events; product explanations need calmer supporting typography. Chrome effects can lose contrast, faux interface widgets can look actionable, and dense stickers can obscure the actual offer. Barcode and QR motifs must either work and lead to the intended destination or be replaced with clearly decorative marks.

**Original execution recipe.** Choose one subject, short headline, dominant cool field and warm/neon accent. Develop an original metallic, inflated or angular headline treatment. Add only two secondary motif families, aligned to an underlying grid. Test at phone size and remove any decoration competing with the subject or action.

## TIP-02 — Pixel Art

**Visual system.** Square-edged lettering, repeated blocks, game HUD elements, hearts, score-like labels and limited-color imagery recur. Several examples combine continuous-tone photography or detailed illustration with pixel treatments. Describe these as hybrids; a mosaic filter does not make a whole photograph pixel art. Use consistent pixel scale, edge treatment and integer scaling for pixel assets.

**Context and limits.** Arcade cues suit game launches, playful menus, retro events and technology campaigns. Pixel effects may obscure faces, fine product details or small text. Simulated scores, prices and technical figures are graphic content here, not verified information. Avoid borrowing recognizable luxury logos or game assets from these examples.

**Original execution recipe.** Decide whether the image itself is pixel-built or whether pixels are accents around a photograph. Pick a grid, compact palette and focal object. Use plain essential text and original icons at matching pixel scale. Check both full size and downsampled output for uneven blocks and disappearing counters.

## TIP-03 — Expressive Typography

**Visual system.** Oversized, condensed, distorted, cropped or extruded letters become imagery. Red, cream, black, blue and pink provide high contrast; portraits, eye fragments, scribbles and torn paper interrupt the words. Spacing is deliberately tense, with little conventional margin. Display type carries emotion; supporting type carries details with less emphasis.

**Context and limits.** Appropriate for short cultural, fashion or creative messages. Avoid it for long explanations, unfamiliar names, critical instructions or complex multilingual text. Cropping can destroy word recognition; compressed lines can alter reading order. Headlines should survive a glance unless ambiguity is intentional.

**Original execution recipe.** Write a short honest message. Sketch scale contrast, repetition and deformation; select the treatment whose emotion matches the meaning. Add one photograph or texture layer to strengthen that idea. Keep dates, names and actions in a stable reading strip. Validate the words with someone unfamiliar with the draft.

## TIP-04 — Maximalism

**Visual system.** These posters stack portraits or classical busts, paper, flowers, tape, paint, checker patterns, marks and short statements. One large face, headline and accent pair often govern the abundance. Overlaps compress spacing; contrast and size guide attention. Every gap need not be filled.

**Context and limits.** Useful for expressive editorial, cultural programming and energetic self-expression. Density may conflict with calm service brands or precise comparisons. Repeated face occlusion becomes a mannerism; adding more textures cannot rescue weak copy. Check cutouts and exports at real viewing size.

**Original execution recipe.** Assign a focal subject, headline, supporting motif and texture layer. Choose two accent colors plus neutral material. Build from large forms to small marks, reserving one quieter area. Make every added element strengthen the theme or directional flow. Temporarily hide half the small marks; restore only those that measurably improve hierarchy or meaning.

## TIP-05 — Gothic

**Visual system.** Dark fields, cream lettering, red or purple accents, blackletter and fine serifs combine with statues, wings, cathedrals, crowns, thorns and engraving-like linework. Vertical arrangements, arches and ornate borders create ceremony. The board mixes historical, religious, fantasy and contemporary fashion references.

**Context and limits.** Consider a fitting music, fantasy, fashion or cultural brief; the mood can suggest mourning or threat. Small blackletter and thin ornament deteriorate quickly on mobile. Consider religious imagery in context; avoid irrelevant occult decoration.

**Original execution recipe.** Define the intended emotional register before choosing motifs. Start with a strong vertical silhouette and one structural arch or frame. Use cream for essential text, a restrained red/purple accent and dark atmospheric imagery. Pair a short display title with a readable support face. Inspect shadow detail and outline continuity after export, then remove symbolic elements that do not serve the concept.

## TIP-06 — Mixed Media

**Visual system.** Photographic portraits meet halftone, collage, paint, handwriting, newspaper, taped labels and graphic shapes. Black-and-white images often make bright pink, yellow, orange or blue additions easier to control. Scale changes between a large face and small photo fragments create rhythm. Paper seams express construction; depth, light and materials still need consistency.

**Context and limits.** Effective as a direction for expressive portraits, artist campaigns or editorial storytelling. Mixed media can be restrained or loud. Decorative facial marks can alter a person's perceived expression. Use intentional original text; random newspaper fragments may introduce unrelated claims or offensive language.

**Original execution recipe.** Start with a clean portrait. Add two media, one large secondary shape and a few annotations. Decide which edges are clean, torn or brushed. Review the portrait before and after the added layers, checking recognition, expression, hierarchy and the truth of every readable fragment.

## TIP-07 — “Neoclassical” source board

**Visual system and naming.** The heading groups contemporary collages of classical busts, historical architecture, painted figures, roses, marble, gold ornament, thin frames and high-contrast serif typography. These examples reference **multiple historical periods**. Describe this as contemporary collage with historical art references; not every painting, cathedral, costume or serif is Neoclassical. One example explicitly carries a Renaissance label.

Cream, navy, black, gold and occasional scarlet support a cultivated editorial mood. Generous spacing, narrow rules and quiet margins give several examples restraint. Gold is often a flat accent or image texture, not evidence of actual metallic printing.

**Context, limits and recipe.** Consider this direction for an art program, cultural editorial or carefully positioned luxury product. Begin with a documented, cleared historical reference and explain why it belongs to the concept. Pair one large art fragment with contemporary typography and a small accent. Use one frame system and give secondary details room. Verify any dates, names or historical labels independently; inspect thin strokes and muted contrast in the intended medium.

## TIP-08 — Scrapbook

**Visual system.** Torn and cut paper, ransom-style letter combinations, newspaper, grayscale photo fragments, eye crops, tape, crowns, chess pieces and red/black/cream palettes evoke a constructed page. Slight rotations and overlapping borders supply tactile rhythm. Effective examples repeat a material language and preserve an anchor subject. Paper thickness, shadows and edge roughness should feel mutually consistent.

**Context and limits.** Useful for personal narratives, rebellious editorial, music or a deliberately handmade campaign. Fragmented faces or judgmental words can feel accusatory or chaotic. Ransom typography slows recognition; chess and royal motifs bring meanings that may not suit the message. Visible historical names do not establish endorsements or quotations.

**Original execution recipe.** Choose a short story, hero photograph, two supporting fragments and cut-letter title. Limit paper rotations to a controlled range and repeat one accent color. Add tape or marks only where they help group or direct. Read every fragment in sequence to check that the collage communicates the intended story.

## TIP-09 — Bento Grid

**Panel geometry.** This board uses two columns and three rows. The identifiers below follow reading order: TL = upper left; TM = upper right; TR = middle left; BL = middle right; BM = lower left; BR = lower right. Identifiers follow reading order rather than physical position.

**Visual system.** Unequal rounded rectangles share gutters, corner radii and edge alignments. Dark panels use neon lime, violet and cyan; lighter panels use cream, pink and orange. Photos, statistics, charts, logos, product objects and short statements occupy distinct cells. Tile size should reflect priority; related tiles need meaningful groups.

**Context, limits and recipe.** Useful for feature summaries, portfolio overviews and compact dashboards. The pictured ratings, users, revenue, prices and percentages are **illustrative reference content**, not business facts. Build the information model first: one lead message, two to four supporting points and optional details. Establish a small column grid, consistent gutters and a radius scale. Allocate larger tiles to higher-priority content, then design mobile reading order and focus behavior where interactive. Use real data or conspicuously labeled placeholders. Check content fit; card polish must not make trivial and critical information appear equally important.

## Practical use within BeyondArtist

Use these boards as options, not an effects checklist. Pick a reference because its communication mechanism fits the brief: an expressive headline for a short emotional message, modular cards for comparable information, or carefully selected collage fragments for a narrative. Combining directions requires a shared hierarchy and color logic.

Cross-references in the BeyondArtist Design Memory visual library: **FND-038–044** cover typography fundamentals; **FND-083** is a four-example Paper Texture board; **FND-084** is Loud Typography; **FND-085** is Cyber Core; **FND-086** is Micro Doodles. Paper Texture relates to the material choices in Mixed Media and Scrapbook; Loud Typography relates to expressive type; Cyber Core overlaps some digital motifs of Y2K; Micro Doodles helps interpret the annotation layers.

State the audience, message, format and mechanism. Create original work with cleared assets; verify facts, hierarchy, legibility, contrast, cutouts and exports at delivery size.
